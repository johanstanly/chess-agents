Self-Improving AI Chess Agents

Results of the experiment (version 2)
Game replay Watch in 3D

Can an AI chess player learn from its own mistakes, without being retrained?

Two chess agents, Magnus and Hans, are the same AI (Claude Opus, thinking effort "high") with the same instructions. The only difference: after every game, Magnus studies his three worst moves (pointed out by the Stockfish chess engine), writes rules into a notebook, and keeps a library of his past mistakes. Before every move he re-reads the notebook and two similar past mistakes. Hans has no memory at all: every move, and every game, starts from zero.

An arbiter program checks that every move is legal. They played 12 games against each other (6 openings, each played twice with the colours swapped), and both played the weak Stockfish level 0 at three checkpoints: before any games, after 6, and after 12.

What we found

  1. The notebook made a clear difference head to head. Magnus beat an identical player without a notebook , and had the higher accuracy in games. In version 1 (Claude Sonnet at low thinking effort, a simpler notebook) the same matchup was even: .
  2. But Magnus did not keep getting better. His average accuracy was in games 1–6 and in games 7–12. The notebook looks like a one-off boost, not steady improvement. He even outplayed Hans in game 1, before he had written a single rule, so part of the gap is luck.
  3. He really used it. Magnus said a notebook rule applied on of his moves. His final rules are mostly safety checks ("list every enemy check and capture before you move", "play each capture sequence to the end") that target the exact blunders that cost him games.
  4. The Stockfish checkpoints cannot prove anything on their own. Magnus stayed level () while Hans dropped (). But Hans has no memory, so he cannot have changed: his drop is pure chance. That shows how much two games can swing, and Magnus's changes are smaller than that swing.
  5. The notebook did not transfer to an unfamiliar kind of position. Every opening before the Queen's Gambit Declined began 1.e4, so his rules were written from open, tactical games. In the slower 1.d4 positions, Magnus played no better than Hans (about 76% vs 75% accuracy), and he lost 4 of his 5 games with it, counting the checkpoints.

Bottom line: memory-based learning (a notebook the agent writes and reads itself, with no retraining) gave a real edge in this experiment, but the evidence is suggestive, not proven: with 12 games, a result like this would happen by luck roughly 1 time in 10. Proving it would take around 30–40 games.

How it was run

  • Model: , run through Claude Code on a Pro plan (no paid API). Tools were switched off, so the agents could only read the position and answer.
  • Each move: the agent got the board, the position code (FEN), the moves so far and the list of legal moves. No hints about attacks or best moves. Magnus also got his notebook and two similar past mistakes.
  • Move protocol: read the notebook (Magnus only); calculate at least two candidate moves and the opponent's best reply to each; play the move.
  • After each game: Stockfish found Magnus's three worst moves and how they were punished. Magnus rewrote his whole notebook (at most 15 rules) and his mistakes and blunders went into the library.
  • Locked settings: the model, effort and instruction files were fingerprinted before game 1, so nothing could change mid-experiment.
  • Scale: 24 games over 5 days, about requests to Claude. Opus at high effort used a whole week of the Pro plan in about 3 days.
